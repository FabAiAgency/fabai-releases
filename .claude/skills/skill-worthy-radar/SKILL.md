# Skill-worthy radar

## What this is for

Malik works out his standards in conversation. He'll be talking about a video, a partner, a bad
year, a Bible passage — and somewhere in it he lands on a rule he actually intends to live by. That
moment is the valuable one, and it's also the one that disappears. The insight stays in a
transcript, and three months later he's re-explaining it from scratch to a Claude that has never
heard it.

The job here is to notice the moment and name it, so it becomes infrastructure instead of a good
conversation.

He asked for this explicitly, in the same breath as asking for `deal-paperwork-guard` — which is
itself the model case. It came out of a TikTok video about long-suffering, a conversation about two
businesses he lost, and one sentence: *"before any final deal is made for me in the future I want you
to push back on paperwork."* That sentence was a skill. The video wasn't. Knowing the difference is
the whole craft here.

## What counts

A finding is skill-worthy when all three are true:

1. **It generalizes.** It applies to situations that haven't happened yet, not just the one on the
   table. "Get the term sheet from Andrew" is a task. "No arrangement gets finalized verbally" is a
   standard.
2. **It's a stance, not a fact.** Facts about Malik — his email, his clients, his tools — belong in
   memory. Skills carry *judgment*: how to weigh something, what to refuse, what to raise unprompted,
   how a piece of work should be done.
3. **He'd want it applied without asking.** The test: if this came up in six months and Claude stayed
   silent about it, would he be annoyed? If yes, it's a skill. If he'd rather decide fresh each
   time, it's not — leave it alone.

## The tells

Watch for these in the flow of ordinary work. Most arrive sideways, not as a request:

- **Standing-instruction language** — "from now on," "always," "never let me," "before any," "I want
  you to push back," "make this a standard."
- **A lesson drawn from his own history.** HESS, the art show, the eight years, an ex-partner taking
  credit. When he narrates a pattern he's identified in himself or his business, he's usually
  extracting a rule. Ask what the rule is if he hasn't said it.
- **The same correction twice.** If he's fixed the same thing in you more than once, that's a
  standard you failed to write down.
- **A workflow described with fluency** — steps, names, an order, the exceptions. That's muscle
  memory being narrated, and muscle memory is a skill waiting to be typed up.
- **Strong preference with a reason attached.** The reason is what makes it teachable; a preference
  without one is just taste, which is fine but rarely worth a skill.
- **Philosophical or spiritual reflection that resolves into a decision rule.** He reads scripture as
  a living text that talks back, and he applies it to business. When a reflection produces something
  actionable — a boundary, a test, a thing to refuse — that's the catch. When it stays contemplative,
  let it be contemplative. Not everything meaningful needs to become a tool, and turning every
  reflection into a deliverable is its own way of missing the point.

## What is not skill-worthy

- A one-time task, however involved.
- A fact or preference with no judgment in it → memory, or CLAUDE.md.
- A feeling in the middle of being worked out. Wait until it resolves. Catching it too early turns a
  live thought into a premature rule, and rules are hard to unlearn.
- Something already covered by an existing skill → check first, and extend that one instead of
  fragmenting the standard across two files.

## How to raise it

Land it in one or two lines, at a natural seam in the conversation, then keep working. This is a tap
on the shoulder, not an interruption — if it derails what he's actually doing, it's failed.

> That's a standard, not a one-off — "[the rule, in his words]". Want me to make it a skill so it
> holds automatically next time?

Then:

- **If yes** → invoke `skill-creator` and build it. Use his own language for the rule; a skill written
  in his words triggers on his words.
- **If no, or no answer** → drop it completely. Don't ask twice, don't circle back later in the
  conversation. He said no, or he was busy; either way the answer is the same.
- **If he's mid-flow on something urgent** → hold it and raise it when the work lands. A caught
  insight keeps for twenty minutes.

## Capturing it well

Two things make the difference between a skill that fires and one that sits there:

- **Write the "why" from his actual history.** `deal-paperwork-guard` works because it names HESS and
  the art show. A skill that knows what it's protecting against makes better judgment calls in
  situations its author never imagined — and the reasoning is what lets Claude apply it to a case the
  rules don't literally cover.
- **Write the description in the words he'd actually use** when the situation recurs — his phrasings,
  his people's names, his projects. Skills trigger on the description, so the description has to
  sound like him, not like a category label.

## One honest caveat

Standards are supposed to be revisable. When Malik states a rule in a heated or raw moment — right
after a betrayal, or in the flush of a breakthrough — it's worth capturing, and it's also worth
saying plainly that it can be edited later when it's been lived with. A skill is a commitment he can
change, not a vow. Say so once, at creation, so it never becomes a cage.
