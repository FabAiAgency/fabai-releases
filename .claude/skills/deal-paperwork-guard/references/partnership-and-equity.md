# Partnerships, co-founders, and revenue splits

The highest-stakes category and the one that matches Malik's history. Everything here exists because
a partnership that is never written down defaults to whoever is louder, whoever holds the account,
and whoever tells the story first.

## Non-negotiables

**Contribution, stated concretely.** Not "we're both all in." Write what each person actually
delivers: hours, capital, clients, equipment, specific deliverables. Vague contribution is how one
partner ends up doing the work while the other takes the shine — the exact art-show failure.

**Performance conditions on the split.** A partner's share should be tied to what they said they'd
contribute. If they stop delivering, the agreement should say what happens: reduced share, buyout,
or dissolution. Without this, a partner who stops working keeps earning forever and the only remedy
is for Malik to walk away from his own business.

**Vesting.** If ownership is involved, it vests over time (a 4-year vest with a 1-year cliff is the
common shape) rather than landing fully on day one. Someone who leaves in month three should not own
a quarter of the company. This single clause would have changed the art show.

**Ownership of the brand and the assets.** Name it explicitly: the business name, domains, social
accounts, client list, equipment, footage/photo archives, software, and designs. Say who owns each
one during the partnership and who keeps it after. If Malik built the brand, the brand should be
his, licensed to the venture — not owned by the venture.

**Credit and attribution.** See `../assets/credit-and-attribution-clause.md`. Do not let this be
"obvious." It was obvious both previous times, too.

**Decision rights.** List the decisions that need both signatures (taking on debt, signing leases,
hiring, changing the split, selling) versus what each person decides alone in their lane. Deadlock
needs a resolution mechanism, even if it's just "Malik decides" — a coin-flip rule beats a stalemate.

**Money mechanics.** Whose bank account, who has access, who invoices clients, who signs checks, how
draws work, when the split is calculated, and who pays expenses. A partner who controls the account
controls the partnership regardless of what the split says.

**Exit and dissolution.** Notice period, buyout formula (or at minimum a method for setting a price),
what happens to clients and work in flight, who keeps the name, and non-solicit of clients and staff
after leaving. Also: what triggers a forced exit — non-performance, misconduct, going dark.

## Questions to actually ask Malik

- If this person walked away in six months, what would they be entitled to take with them?
- If they stopped doing their part but stayed, what could you do about it? (If the answer is
  "nothing but quit," the agreement is missing a performance clause.)
- Whose name is the client relationship in? Who do the clients think they hired?
- If this blows up, who keeps the business name?
- Are you paying them, or are they a partner? Those are different documents and the difference is
  usually money vs. ownership — don't blur it.

## The cheap version

A signed one-page term sheet answering the five questions beats an unsigned "real" contract that
never gets drafted. Get the term sheet signed this week; have the attorney formalize it after.
